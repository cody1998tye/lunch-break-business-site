THE LUNCH-BREAK MATH

Thirty minutes a day is enough to make your first $500, as long as you spend it making something and you protect it like a shift you clocked into. This chapter shows you where those minutes are hiding in your day and how to keep them.

The math

30 minutes on every workday lunch break adds up to 2.5 hours a week. Over a year that's about 130 hours, more than three full 40-hour work weeks. Most people who say they don't have time are picturing a free Saturday. You don't need one.

That's how I built all of this. I grew my Threads account from zero to over 10,000 followers in about 150 days, posting in 30-minute windows, from my phone. My first offer sold 11 times before I'd built it. None of it happened on a day off.

"I have always found that the best time for me to get work done on my business is during my breaks at work. I get two 15 minute breaks and a 30 minute lunch. When I started out a lot of that time went to doing something on my business. After my kids go to bed is another great time — usually 8pm. Nowadays I mainly work on my 30 minute lunch break, a little after the kids are down and in some gaps I have throughout my workday. It's all about finding what works for you the best and sticking to it every single day." — Cody

Where the 30 minutes hides

Your time is already there. It's just in pieces. Most working men have some mix of these:

Window Usually about Best for
Parking lot before your shift10 to 15 minWriting one post or one idea
Morning or afternoon break10 to 15 minReplying to people, quick edits
Lunch20 to 30 minYour main task for the day
After the kids are down30 to 60 minBigger work: a sales page, delivering an order

You don't need all four. You need one you can count on every workday, plus a backup for the days that one gets wrecked.

One rule: the time comes from dead time, never from your family. Scrolling time, waiting time, sitting-in-the-truck time. If your plan needs you to miss dinner, it's the wrong plan.

Why short sessions work

  • Small wins keep you coming back. Researchers Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer studied about 12,000 daily diary entries from people at work. The thing that lifted people's motivation most on a given day was making progress on something meaningful, even a small step. Thirty minutes gives you a win almost every day.
  • A short window forces you to pick one thing. With a whole Saturday you'd "work on the business." With 30 minutes you write the post or you don't.
  • The start is where you lose. When companies made 401(k) sign-up automatic instead of opt-in, one well-known study (Madrian and Shea, 2001) saw participation jump from about 49% to 86%. Same people, easier start. If you have to decide what to do when lunch begins, half your break is gone before you start.

The three rules of a lunch-break session

  1. Decide the task before the break starts. Write it the night before or on your morning break. When lunch hits, you open the note and go.
  2. One task per session. The session ends when that task is done or the time's up, whichever comes first.
  3. Leave yourself a parking spot. Before you put the phone down, write one line: the very next step. Tomorrow you start there instead of figuring out where you were.

When you miss a day

You will. Life happens to all of us. A kid gets sick, a manager pulls you into a meeting on your lunch, or you just need a day away from the internet and with your family.

The danger is what happens next. Diet and habit research calls it the "what-the-hell effect": one slip turns into "I already blew it," and the whole plan gets dropped. That's how most people's 30-day plans die on day 9.

  • Never miss twice. One missed day is life. Two in a row is the start of a new habit, and it's the wrong one.
  • Let yourself off the hook. Research on procrastination found that students who forgave themselves for putting off studying procrastinated less the next time. Beating yourself up only teaches you to avoid the thing that made you feel bad.

What 30 minutes can't do

It won't make $500 this week, and it won't work if you spend it "researching." Watching videos about making money online feels like work and produces nothing, which is the Chapter 1 loop in a smaller package. During your 30 minutes, you make things. You consume on your own time, if at all.

Your task: Find your 30 minutes

Take 10 minutes. You'll need your phone's alarm app.

Step 1. Map one normal workday

Time What I'm doing Phone in my hand? Free minutes

Step 2. Pick your main window

Look at the free minutes column. Pick the one window that shows up every workday and that you can protect.

Step 3. Pick a backup window

For the days your main window gets wrecked.

Step 4. Write your if-then plan

"When it's ______ and I'm ______, I'll open ______ and work on this guide for ______ minutes. If that gets wrecked, I'll use ______ instead."

Step 5. Set the alarm

Make a daily alarm for your main window. Label it with your if-then plan so you see it when it goes off.

Do it with AI — copy this prompt into ChatGPT or Claude
You're my time coach. You're walking me through "Find your 30 minutes" from Chapter 2 of Cody Tye's guide "Your First $500 Online." The goal: find one window of up to 30 minutes on every workday that I can protect, plus a backup window, and turn it into an if-then plan I'll actually follow. The time must come from dead time (scrolling, waiting, sitting around), never from time with my family.

About me:
- My job and shift: [example: warehouse lead, 6am to 3pm, Monday to Friday]
- My breaks: [example: two 15-minute breaks and a 30-minute lunch]
- Family stuff I won't move: [example: dinner at 6, kids' bedtime at 8:30]
- Anything else that eats my time: [example: second job, commute, kids' sports]

How to run this:
1. Ask me ONE question at a time. Wait for my answer before you ask the next one.
2. Walk through one normal workday with me in order, from waking up to bed. For each part of the day, ask what I'm doing, whether my phone is usually in my hand, and how many minutes are really free. If I say "I'm busy," ask what I'm actually doing.
3. Ask how much time I spend scrolling or watching videos on a normal day. Help me see which of those minutes I could take back.
4. Show me my day as a table: Time | What I'm doing | Phone in my hand? | Free minutes.
5. Help me pick my MAIN window (shows up every workday, I can protect it) and a BACKUP window. Tell me if a window I pick looks unrealistic and why.
6. Help me write my if-then plan: "When it's ___ and I'm ___, I'll open ___ and work on this guide for ___ minutes. If that gets wrecked, I'll use ___ instead."
7. Give me my feedback:
   - How many minutes a week I really have, and what that adds up to over a year
   - The biggest time leak you noticed
   - Anything in my plan that's likely to break, and how to fix it
   - My day table and my if-then plan, written out clean so I can screenshot them
   - The exact label to put on my daily alarm

Rules:
- Keep every message short. I'm reading on my phone on a break.
- Be direct and honest. Don't flatter me.
- Don't move to the next step until the current one is done.
- Never suggest taking time from my family or my sleep.
- Don't suggest new business ideas, tools or courses.
