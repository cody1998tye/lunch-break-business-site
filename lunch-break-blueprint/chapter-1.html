WHY YOU HAVEN'T MADE A DOLLAR YET

You keep starting over because starting over is what gets sold, and finishing is the only part that pays you. This chapter shows you the loop so you can spot it the next time it shows up.

The loop

  1. A new promise shows up. A course, a video, a guy in a rented Lambo.
  2. You buy it, and buying feels like progress.
  3. The work gets hard. The new feeling wears off, and finishing means someone could see it and judge it.
  4. Another new promise shows up. You start over.

Step 2 is the sneaky one. A 2017 study (Gonçalves and colleagues) found that buying a product tied to a goal can feel almost like taking action on the goal. The purchase scratches the itch the work was supposed to scratch. So you feel busy, you feel like you're moving, and your bank account says otherwise.

"I was in this loop too. I watched hundreds of hours on videos for dropshipping, affiliate marketing and content operating. I would take the free knowledge and try to do it myself but would get absolutely nowhere and then the cycle would continue. Once bills were due the thought of 'I need to make more money' would come to mind and back to YouTube and free guides I would go. I even invested into some affiliate courses back in 2025 and would feel good buying them but I would read it and implement some things then have a bad or busy day and never look at it again." — Cody

Why the hard part hits right where it does

  • The real timeline is longer than the one in your head. In a classic study (Buehler, Griffin and Ross, 1994), students guessed their thesis would take about 34 days. It took about 56. When week three feels slow, you're right on schedule.
  • Quitting makes you feel better right now. Procrastination research (Steel, 2007) shows we put things off to fix our mood in the moment. Opening a shiny new course feels better than rewriting a sales page nobody's bought from yet.
  • A finished thing can be judged. While your offer is half-built, it can't fail. Once it's posted, it can. Plenty of people stay "almost ready" for years to avoid that moment.
  • Fresh starts feel clean. Researchers (Dai, Milkman and Riis) found people get a real motivation bump from new beginnings: Mondays, the first of the month, New Year's. Every new program is sold as a fresh start, and that's on purpose.

What the people who get paid do differently

  • They pick one thing and protect it. One offer, one platform, until it pays.
  • They decide when and where ahead of time. "When it's 12:15 and I'm in my truck, I write one post." Research on if-then plans like this (Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006) found they make people meaningfully more likely to follow through.
  • They give the work a deadline someone else can see. A buyer waiting on you beats any amount of motivation. Pick a date to complete something you know you want to finish and announce it publicly, just the thought of people knowing you'd do something can be enough for you to finish.
  • They sell before they build. That's how I made my first sales. I put up a sales page and a buy link for something that didn't exist yet and gave myself 11 days. Eleven people bought. I was scared nobody would pay for something they couldn't have right away. Then I had eleven reasons to finish, and I delivered two days early. You'll do your own version in Chapter 6.

Your tool: The Restart Audit

Take 10 minutes. Be honest. Nobody sees this but you.

Step 1

What I bought or started About when How far I got Why I stopped

Step 2: Find your quitting point.

Look at the last column. Did you usually stop when it got boring, when it got hard, or right before you had to show it to someone? Circle the one that shows up most. That's the moment to watch for.

Step 3: Write your 30-day rule.

Fill in the blanks and screenshot it: "For the next 30 days, I'm finishing this guide and nothing else. When I feel the urge to buy something new, I'll ________ instead."

Do it with AI — copy this prompt into ChatGPT or Claude
You're my accountability coach. You're walking me through the Restart Audit from Chapter 1 of Cody Tye's guide "Your First $500 Online." The goal: figure out why I keep starting over, find the moment I usually quit, and write a 30-day rule I'll actually stick to.

About me:
- My job and schedule: [example: warehouse lead, 6am to 3pm, 30-minute lunch]
- Time I can give this each day: [example: 30 minutes on lunch]
- Things I've bought or started to make money online in the last 2 years: [list them, rough is fine]

How to run this:
1. Ask me ONE question at a time. Wait for my answer before you ask the next one.
2. Go through each thing I listed, one at a time. For each one, ask about when I started it, how far I got, roughly what it cost me, and why I stopped. If my answer is vague (like "I got busy"), ask what was actually going on that week.
3. When we've covered everything, show me my audit as a table with these columns: What I bought or started | About when | How far I got | Why I stopped.
4. Help me find my quitting point. Ask whether I usually stopped when it got boring, when it got hard, or right before I had to show it to someone. Then tell me the pattern YOU see in my answers, even if it's different from what I picked.
5. Help me write my 30-day rule: "For the next 30 days, I'm finishing this guide and nothing else. When I feel the urge to buy something new, I'll ______ instead." Make sure the replacement is specific and takes under 5 minutes on my phone.
6. Give me my feedback:
   - The pattern you saw
   - The exact moment I need to watch for
   - What this loop has cost me in time and money, using only the numbers I gave you (don't guess)
   - One honest thing I need to hear
   - My audit table and my 30-day rule, written out clean so I can screenshot them

Rules:
- Keep every message short. I'm reading on my phone on a break.
- Be direct and honest. Don't flatter me.
- Don't move to the next step until the current one is done.
- Don't suggest new business ideas, tools or courses. This is about finishing.

Start now with your first question.
